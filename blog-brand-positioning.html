Why most brand positioning
fails — and what great
brands do differently

Most companies confuse their product features with their brand positioning. Great brands don't sell what they do — they sell what their customer becomes.

Walk into any boardroom in Nairobi and ask the leadership team to define their brand positioning. You'll get answers like "we're the most reliable," "best quality for the price," or "customer-centric." These aren't positions. They're aspirations — and every competitor in the room says the same thing.

Brand positioning failure is almost universal. And it's not because founders and marketers don't care about their brand. It's because they're solving the wrong problem. They're trying to communicate what they do better, when they should be answering a far more powerful question: what does the customer become when they choose us?

The feature trap

The most common positioning mistake is leading with features. "We have 24/7 support." "Our software is AI-powered." "We source locally." These are inputs. They're relevant — but they're not positions.

A feature becomes a position only when it maps to a transformation the customer cares about deeply. "24/7 support" becomes a position when your customer is a business owner who has lost sleep worrying about system downtime. Then it's not a feature — it's peace of mind. That's a position.

"Great brands don't sell products. They sell identity. The question isn't 'what do we offer?' — it's 'who does the customer become when they choose us?'"

The five positioning failures

1. Positioning to everyone

The fastest way to have no position is to try to appeal to everyone. Positioning requires exclusion. A position that resonates with a 35-year-old Nairobi entrepreneur will not resonate with a Mombasa retailer in the same way — and trying to write for both means you write for neither.

2. Copying the category leader

If you position yourself as "the affordable Safaricom" or "the reliable alternative to X," you've already lost. You're spending your brand budget making the leader more famous. Challengers win by redefining the category, not by playing second fiddle in someone else's frame.

3. Leading with process, not promise

Customers don't buy how you work — they buy what working with you delivers. "We use a three-stage discovery process" is not a positioning statement. "We get campaigns live in 48 hours" is. One describes you. The other describes the customer's experience.

4. Positioning that can't be proven

If you claim "the best marketing results in Kenya" but can't back it with case studies, data, or social proof, the claim does more damage than good. Every unsubstantiated superlative erodes trust. Position around what you can prove — and let the proof do the selling.

5. Forgetting the internal audience

Brand positioning isn't just external. Every person on your team needs to feel and live the position in how they answer the phone, write an email, or handle a complaint. A positioning statement pinned to a boardroom wall that no one lives is worse than no positioning at all — it breeds cynicism.

How great brands actually position

The most durable brand positions share three characteristics: they're customer-centric, they're defensible, and they're specific.

Customer-centric

Start with your best customer — not your average customer, but the one for whom you provide the most disproportionate value. What's their deepest frustration? What does winning look like for them? Your position should feel like it was written specifically for that person.

Defensible

Can you own this position over time? Is it rooted in a genuine capability, a real structural advantage, or a deeply held belief that competitors can't easily replicate? Speed, specialisation, and perspective are often more defensible than product features.

Specific

Vague positions don't stick. "We help businesses grow" could be said by anyone. "We get funded startups to Series B faster than any agency in East Africa" is specific, audacious, and memorable. Specificity signals confidence — and confidence is magnetic.

A practical framework to start today

Here's the positioning template we use at Avalor for every new client engagement:

  • For [specific customer segment]
  • Who [have this specific problem or desire]
  • Our brand is [category/type of solution]
  • That [delivers this specific outcome]
  • Unlike [key alternative or competitor]
  • We [have this unique differentiator that makes the outcome believable]

Fill this in honestly — not aspirationally. The answer to "unlike" should make someone at a competitor uncomfortable. The answer to "we" should be something you can prove in under 30 seconds.

Test it on your best customer. If they nod immediately and say "that's exactly why I chose you," you've got it. If they look politely confused, keep going.

Brand positioning isn't a tagline exercise. It's a strategic decision about where you'll compete, who you'll serve, and what you'll be willing to give up. Done right, it becomes the engine behind every campaign, every hire, and every product decision you make.
