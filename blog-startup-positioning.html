Positioning your startup
in a crowded market:
a practical guide

With more startups launching across East Africa than ever, differentiation is survival. This is our step-by-step framework for carving out an undeniable position.

Kenya saw over 400 tech startups launch in 2025 alone. Across East Africa, that number is closer to 1,200. More competition, smarter investors, and more sophisticated customers mean that "we're building X for the African market" is no longer a position — it's a category.

The startups that win aren't always the ones with the best technology or the biggest team. They're the ones that own a clear, specific, memorable position in the mind of their customer before anyone else does.

Here is the exact framework we use with every early-stage client at Avalor.

Step 1: Know your enemy

Before you position yourself, map every alternative your customer has. Not just your direct competitors — every alternative, including doing nothing. If you're a B2B SaaS, the alternative might be Excel spreadsheets. If you're a DTC brand, the alternative might be the unbranded version sold at the supermarket.

For each alternative, list: what it does well, what it does poorly, and what customers say about it online (reviews, Twitter, Reddit, community forums). You're looking for the consistent complaint — the thing people wish the current solution did differently. That's your opening.

Step 2: Find the underserved job

Customers don't buy products — they hire them to do a job. Clayton Christensen's Jobs to Be Done framework is still the most useful positioning tool in existence. Ask: what is the customer really hiring this product to do? What progress are they trying to make in their life or business?

"People don't want a quarter-inch drill. They want a quarter-inch hole. But really, they want a shelf on the wall. But really, they want their spouse to think they're capable."

Go deep. The functional job ("send money quickly") is almost always less differentiated than the emotional job ("feel in control of my finances") or the social job ("be seen as someone who is financially savvy"). The deeper the job, the more powerful the position.

Step 3: Pick your axis of differentiation

Every market has a dominant axis on which competitors compete — usually price or features. The best positions choose a different axis entirely. In a market competing on price, compete on speed. In a market competing on features, compete on simplicity. In a market competing on breadth, compete on depth for one specific segment.

  • Axis 1 — Specialisation: "We only work with Series A fintech startups in East Africa" is more powerful than "we work with all startups"
  • Axis 2 — Speed: "Campaigns live in 48 hours" beats "great results" every time for an impatient founder
  • Axis 3 — Certainty: "We show you exactly what we'll do before you pay" removes the risk that stops most buyers
  • Axis 4 — Community: Being the brand associated with a specific identity or tribe ("for Kenyan founders building global companies")

Step 4: Write the position statement

Use this template — fill it in honestly, not aspirationally:

  • For [hyper-specific customer segment]
  • Who struggle with [their deepest, most specific frustration]
  • We are the only [category + differentiator]
  • That [delivers this specific, measurable outcome]
  • Because [this is why we can deliver what others can't]

The word "only" is important. If you can't say "we are the only X that Y," your position isn't specific enough yet. Keep narrowing until you can say it truthfully.

Step 5: Test it before you commit

Before you redesign your website, rewrite your pitch deck, or spend money on advertising, test the position in conversations. Call your 10 best current customers and pitch the new position as if you just launched. Do they lean in or look confused? Does it resonate with your best customers specifically?

The right position will feel slightly uncomfortable — like you're excluding people. That discomfort is correct. A position that appeals to everyone appeals to no one.

Common mistakes East African startups make

  • Positioning to the entire continent when you should own one country first
  • Leading with technology ("AI-powered", "blockchain-based") instead of outcome
  • Copying Western brand language that doesn't resonate locally
  • Changing position every 6 months because investors gave different feedback
  • Confusing "we serve everyone" with "we're accessible" — they're not the same
